Final Notes on Replicator-NashMTL Toy Design, Settings, and Current Evidence

Overview

- This note is meant to be the most detailed high-signal description of the current algorithm design and toy validation state.
- It focuses on what was actually built and verified in the toy harness.
- It is intentionally more complete than a paper-ready summary and is meant to preserve design rationale before later compression.

Core Goal of the Design

- The purpose of the modified method is not to replace Nash-MTL.
- The purpose is to augment Nash-MTL with a slower outer scheduler that can adjust long-run task emphasis over training.
- The inner Nash update is meant to preserve step-level multi-task bargaining behavior.
- The outer replicator layer is meant to respond to training dynamics over time:
  - which tasks are under-improving
  - which tasks are underperforming relative to others
  - which tasks may need more long-run support

High-Level Structure of the Algorithm

- The method can be understood as a two-level weighting system.

- Inner level:
  - Nash-MTL computes task weights every optimization step.
  - These weights come from the bargaining-based optimization already present in the repository.
  - This component produces the raw `nash_weights`.

- Outer level:
  - A replicator-style scheduler maintains task shares over time.
  - This component updates more slowly than the Nash step.
  - It is not intended to fully override Nash.
  - Instead, it provides a slower-moving notion of which tasks deserve relatively more or less influence.

- Final combination:
  - The final optimization weights are obtained by modulating the Nash weights with the replicator shares.
  - This produces `final_weights`.
  - So the effective training signal is:
    - Nash determines the local bargaining solution
    - replicator nudges that solution over time

Intended Behavioral Story

- Nash-MTL should continue to handle per-step conflict balancing.
- The replicator scheduler should capture slower task-level trends.
- The outer scheduler should not collapse to one task under ordinary settings.
- The final weights should differ from plain Nash when the scheduler is meaningfully active.
- The method should ideally produce:
  - smoother adaptation than direct raw-loss heuristics
  - more interpretable long-run task allocation
  - stability without one-hot degeneration

Why the Original Toy Setup Was Not Enough

- Originally, the toy trainer did not pass an explicit scheduler signal into `replicator_nashmtl`.
- That meant the method fell back to a nearly symmetric default behavior.
- In practice, this caused:
  - `replicator_shares` to stay effectively uniform
  - the outer scheduler to look inactive
  - the toy result to reflect mostly Nash behavior rather than the new design

- As a result, the toy experiment was not a meaningful validation of the outer scheduler.

What Was Changed in the Toy Harness

- The toy trainer was turned into a scheduler-validation harness rather than just a Pareto-front demo.

- Main changes:
  - explicit `payoff_matrix` construction at every step
  - trainer-side EMA tracking for task losses
  - multiple scheduler-signal modes
  - saved histories and scheduler plots
  - automatic checks for movement, normalization, and collapse
  - baseline comparison mode
  - representative debug initialization preset

Scheduler Signal Design

- The toy trainer now supports multiple scheduler signal modes:
  - `none`
  - `loss_ratio`
  - `loss_gap`
  - `improvement`
  - `qm9_proxy`

- The final preferred mode is `qm9_proxy`.

Why `qm9_proxy` was introduced

- The toy setup is not supposed to remain a purely geometric toy.
- The goal was to make the toy-side scheduler input resemble the kind of statistics available in a real training loop like QM9.
- The toy harness now mimics experiment-side quantities such as:
  - current per-task relative loss
  - smoothed loss improvement over time
  - relative task underperformance

- This makes the toy experiment more useful as a proxy for real adaptive weighting behavior.

Detailed Interpretation of `qm9_proxy`

- The `qm9_proxy` signal is a blended signal, not a single raw statistic.

- It combines:
  - current task underperformance
  - EMA-smoothed under-improvement
  - relative task imbalance

- Conceptually:
  - if a task is performing worse than the others, it should receive more attention
  - if a task is improving more slowly than the others, it should receive more attention
  - if both effects agree, the scheduler should move more strongly

- The signal is intentionally smoother than direct raw-loss control.

Why EMA-Smoothed Improvement Was Important

- Raw instantaneous losses were too noisy and too sensitive to scale.
- Relative improvement over time better matches the intended evolutionary scheduling story.
- EMA smoothing helps by:
  - reducing step-to-step volatility
  - making the scheduler less reactive to transient fluctuations
  - giving a more stable notion of under-improvement

- A key stability fix was using the absolute EMA magnitude as a normalization denominator and clamping the improvement signal.
- This avoided exploding ratios when losses were negative or near zero.

Why the Replicator Should Modulate Nash Instead of Dominating It

- A key design choice is that the replicator shares should not directly replace the Nash weights.
- If they dominated the weighting rule, the method would stop resembling Nash-MTL and would instead become a different scheduler entirely.
- The current design preserves the intended hierarchy:
  - Nash provides the base bargaining solution
  - replicator alters the relative emphasis without discarding the Nash structure

- This is important both algorithmically and narratively:
  - algorithmically, it constrains the outer scheduler
  - narratively, it supports the claim that the method is an augmentation of Nash-MTL rather than a full replacement

Observable Quantities in the Saved Scheduler Plots

- The saved scheduler figure shows three levels of behavior:

- `replicator_shares`
  - the outer scheduler state
  - indicates which tasks are being emphasized over the long run

- `nash_weights`
  - the raw bargaining solution before outer modulation
  - useful for seeing what plain Nash would prefer

- `final_weights`
  - the actual optimization weights after combining Nash and replicator effects
  - these are the most important weights for real optimization behavior

- This separation is important because it lets us see:
  - whether the outer scheduler is moving
  - whether it is fighting or reinforcing Nash
  - whether the final result is meaningfully different from plain Nash

Representative Toy Cases

- `init=0`
  - this is the strongest nontrivial representative case
  - it consistently shows visible scheduler motion
  - this is the main mechanism-validation case

- `init=1`
  - this is the hard symmetric-origin case
  - earlier versions of the scheduler could collapse here
  - the stabilized design keeps it controlled
  - this is the main stability-validation case

- `init=2`, `init=3`, `init=4`
  - these remained stable but were generally less dramatic
  - they were more useful for checking control than for showcasing adaptation

Old Failure Mode

- An earlier stress-test configuration caused a collapse on `init=1`.
- In that regime:
  - the scheduler became too aggressive
  - `replicator_shares` moved toward effectively one-hot behavior
  - the outer scheduler stopped being a controlled modulator and became a pathological selector

- This failure mode mattered because it revealed:
  - the basic mechanism could move
  - but it could also become unstable under poorly normalized or overly aggressive settings

How the Collapse Was Fixed

- The collapse was addressed by a combination of signal and hyperparameter fixes.

- Signal-level fixes:
  - stable normalization of improvement
  - denominator based on absolute EMA magnitude
  - improvement clipping
  - more controlled payoff shaping

- Hyperparameter-level fixes:
  - gentler replicator learning rate
  - nonzero uniform mixing
  - moderated modulation strength
  - better update frequency choice

- The result is that the hard case is now adaptive but controlled rather than collapsing.

Final Toy Hyperparameters

- Final chosen toy default:
  - `--scheduler-signal qm9_proxy`
  - `--debug-init-preset representative`
  - `--replicator-update-every 1`
  - `--replicator-lr 0.05`
  - `--replicator-uniform-mix 0.05`
  - `--replicator-modulation-strength 0.3`
  - `--toy-payoff-temperature 0.1`
  - `--toy-payoff-gain 5.0`
  - `--n-epochs 220`

Why These Hyperparameters Were Chosen

- `replicator_update_every = 1`
  - among the tested values `1`, `5`, and `10`, this gave the clearest adaptive response
  - `10` was noticeably flatter
  - `5` was intermediate

- `replicator_lr = 0.05`
  - among the tested values `0.02`, `0.05`, and `0.1`
  - `0.02` was too mild
  - `0.1` was more aggressive than necessary
  - `0.05` was the best middle ground

- `replicator_modulation_strength = 0.3`
  - among the tested values `0.1`, `0.3`, and `0.5`
  - `0.1` was too weak
  - `0.5` gave stronger visible effects but was more aggressive than needed for the default
  - `0.3` was the best conservative final choice

- `replicator_uniform_mix = 0.05`
  - prevents the shares from becoming overly brittle
  - still allows visible movement

- `toy_payoff_temperature = 0.1`
  - keeps the scheduler responsive without being hypersensitive

- `toy_payoff_gain = 5.0`
  - produces a visible signal without returning to the old stress-test instability

What Was Verified Empirically on the Toy Setup

- Baseline-vs-replicator comparison:
  - plain Nash-MTL remained flat and non-adaptive
  - replicator-NashMTL produced a controlled change in behavior

- Mechanism validation:
  - the outer scheduler clearly moves on the representative case
  - the final weights differ from normalized Nash on that case

- Stability validation:
  - the old `init=1` collapse can be shown explicitly
  - the repaired `init=1` behavior remains controlled and non-one-hot

- 3-seed confidence pass:
  - the toy behavior is consistent across seeds in the current setup
  - the method appears stable in the toy harness

Phase 4 Ablation Results

- Fast vs slow replicator:
  - `replicator_update_every=1` vs `10`
  - both were stable
  - `1` produced a stronger visible adaptive effect
  - `10` was more sluggish/flatter

- Collapse vs stable comparison:
  - old stress-test `init=1` collapsed
  - new stabilized `init=1` remained normalized and controlled

- Optional signal ablation:
  - `qm9_proxy` vs `loss_gap`
  - under the current fixed representative setup, these were effectively identical
  - this means the signal-ablation story is weaker than the speed/stability story under the current chosen defaults

How to Interpret the Current Toy Evidence

- The toy evidence is strong for:
  - mechanism validation
  - stability validation
  - showing that the scheduler is active
  - showing that collapse can be avoided

- The toy evidence is not strong for:
  - proving benchmark superiority
  - proving real-data generalization
  - proving that replicator-NashMTL is universally better than Nash-MTL

- In other words:
  - the toy story is a method-and-stability story
  - not a final empirical performance story

Claims Not Yet Supported

- That replicator-NashMTL improves benchmark performance on QM9.
- That replicator-NashMTL improves benchmark performance on NYUv2.
- That the new method is empirically superior on real data rather than mainly more stable and more interpretable.

  - `nashmtl`
  - `replicator_nashmtl`

- However, the bounded smoke-test attempts did not produce clean, timely benchmark comparison outputs.
- So the non-toy path is best described as:
  - partially alive
  - not yet giving a clean benchmark figure

Current Best Short Summary

- Nash weights are still computed every step.
- The outer replicator scheduler updates every `k` steps using EMA-smoothed improvement and underperformance signals.
- The replicator modulates the Nash solution rather than replacing it.
- The repaired toy design is substantially more stable than the earlier aggressive version.
- The strongest confirmed improvement so far is stability and controlled adaptive weighting behavior, not benchmark-level empirical superiority.
